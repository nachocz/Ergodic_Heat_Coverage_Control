Closing the loop: ergodic coverage and standoff regulation

EHCC uses the measured error in two decoupled channels: the ergodic controller decides where the source goes, and the standoff controller decides how much heat it injects. Neither uses the thermal model; the simulation in the workspace does.

Where to heat: ergodic coverage (Sec. III)

The heat deficit defines a target density for the motion of the source, recomputed from the measured error at every instant:

\Phi(\vx,t)=\frac{e_+(\vx,t)}{\int_\Omega e_+(\vx,t)\,d\vx+\epsilon}

With the cosine basis \Psi_{\vk}(\vx)=\cos(k_1\pi x)\cos(k_2\pi y) (11), the controller compares the coefficients of \Phi with those of the time-averaged trajectory:

\phi_{\vk}(t)=\int_\Omega\Phi\,\Psi_{\vk}\,d\vx,\qquad c_{\vk}(t)=\frac1t\int_0^t\Psi_{\vk}\big(\vp(\tau)\big)\,d\tau
\E(t)=\sum_{\vk}\Lambda_{\vk}\big(c_{\vk}-\phi_{\vk}\big)^2,\qquad \Lambda_{\vk}=\big(1+\norm{\vk}^2\big)^{-s}
\vu_\tau(t)=-K_g\sum_{\vk}\Lambda_{\vk}\big(c_{\vk}-\phi_{\vk}\big)\nabla_{\vp}\Psi_{\vk}\big(\vp(t)\big)

The source therefore spends more time where more heat is missing. The speed is limited to u_{\max}.

How much heat: the standoff (Sec. IV)

The local deficit at the focal point, e_{\vp}(t)=e_+(\vp(t),t) (17), sets the standoff between \dmax (no deficit, no heating, \eta(\dmax)\approx0) and the adaptive lower bound \dlb(t):

d(t)=\dmax-\frac{\dmax-\dlb(t)}{\emax}\,e_{\vp}(t)

The lower bound integrates the mean error over \ROI, saturated to [\dmin,\dmax]:

\ebar(t)=\frac{1}{|\ROI|}\int_{\ROI}e\,d\vx,\qquad \dot d_{\mathrm{lb}}=-k_I\,\ebar(t)

Both mechanisms act through the gate g=(\dmax-\dlb)/(\dmax-\dmin)\in[0,1] (22), plotted below: g=1 gives the full heating range, g=0 holds the source at \dmax. After over-heating (\ebar<0), g decreases and the surface cools passively.

Try this

  1. Press Run with the default setup (target T4, the benchmark schedule of Sec. VI). \E(t) drops after the first sweeps, and \ebar(t) settles near zero with a spread \sigma_e set by diffusion at the edges of the target.
  2. At 16:40 a hot source (+600 W, 20 s) over-heats the surface: \dlb rises to \dmax (g\to0) and the surface cools passively. At 26:40 a cold sink (−600 W, 60 s) does the opposite. Click on the T map to place your own disturbance, then use the buttons.
  3. Press EHCC-NA (k_I=0 and \dlb\equiv\dmin, the ablation of Sec. VI-B) and run again: with the full heating range and no integral law, the mean error turns negative (over-heating).
  4. Choose T5 or the interleaved plateau: the domain is heterogeneous (metal and rubber), and the controller receives no information about it.
  5. Choose the sole target of the 3D trial and the scanned surface, and follow the frame \vR_s along the trajectory in the 3D view.
  6. Change the conductivity \kappa or the convection h during a run: the loop compensates without any retuning.

Coverage sets where the heat goes, and the integral standoff law sets how much heat enters on average; together they hold \ebar near zero on materials that the controller knows nothing about.

Model and parameters of the simulation

The plant is the heat equation (2) on a 100 × 100 grid over a 0.35 m square (the scanned surface is 0.47 m), with the actuation model (3)–(4), explicit Euler steps of 0.2 s, and Neumann boundaries. The material outside \ROI is an insulating backing (coupling reduced to 0.4). The default values are those of the simulation benchmark of Sec. VI: P=1200 W, \eta\in[0,0.9], \sigma\in[0.02,0.04], d\in[0.05,0.45] m, K=15, s=1.05, K_g=0.4, u_{\max}=0.1 domain lengths per second, and k_I=0.04 (0.02 for the heterogeneous targets).

The code is a port of the Python package ehcc/; tests/test_web_parity.py checks that both give the same logs, and the tests reproduce the EHCC row of Table II for target T4.
